Momentum: scoring runs aren't what they look like

After winning three rallies in a row, Nebraska wins the next one 49.4% of the time — less than its 55.7% on all 85,615 rallies. That isn't a cold streak. The team that wins a rally serves the next one (true on 83,717 of 83,717 consecutive rallies), and serving is the harder side of the net. Winning hands the serve over.

Compare like with like — serves that follow a streak against serves that start a fresh turn, after taking out what a random order of the same rallies produces — and what is left is +0.5 points (95% interval -0.6 to +1.5). The record is big enough to catch an effect of about 1.3 points; it doesn't find one.

Runs of three or more points. Shuffling each set's own rallies into a random order 2,000 times (keeping every final score) produces more runs than really happened, for both teams: winning a rally hands over the serve, which breaks runs up.

Serving harder doesn't pay

Over 47,212 Nebraska serves in 26 seasons, 5.2% were aces and 8.2% errors. When a serve lands and isn't an ace, Nebraska still wins the rally 48.8% of the time — so a missed serve throws that away, and one extra point of error rate has to buy about 0.95 points of ace rate just to break even.

Nebraska's servers get nowhere near it. Comparing servers with each other, the riskier ones buy about 0.34 (95% interval 0.16 to 0.30); following one server from one season to the next, about 0.16 (0.05 to 0.27). Against a serve that never missed and never aced, the serving as a whole is worth -0.013 points a serve — about -626 points over 26 seasons.

How the game changed

Rally scoring arrived in 2001 and the 25-point set in 2008, but the set only changed length once.

Under side-out scoring only the serving team could score, so a set had as many rallies as a rally-scoring set to 30 but less than half the points.

Matches have also slowed down. Nebraska's matches ran at 1.72 rallies a minute in 2000 and 1.43 in 2026 (550 matches print both a duration and a full rally log).

On the bench

When does Nebraska substitute? In the 94 sets of Busboom Kelly 2025-2026 whose substitutions the record lets us read, Nebraska made 394 substitutions. 235 were planned rotation swaps — the same specialist in and out at the same turn of the rotation, set after set — and 82 came with Nebraska up 6 or more late in a set or a set from the match. That leaves 21 like-for-like changes, the only ones that could be a reaction to how a set was going. None of 7 possible triggers the record can see — a player's own errors just before, an opponent run, a stretch of failed side-outs, the score — shows up at those moments more than chance allows once 7 tests are allowed for. The closest is the score itself: Nebraska's margin in the set, in points (2.2 at these changes against 4.1 at comparable moments, p = 0.0072, just short of the 0.0071 needed). With so few, that rules out a big, regular trigger, not a subtle one. The same test does find triggers in Cook 2016-2024 (41 like-for-like changes), so it can find one when one is there.

Do timeouts stop a run? Of the 3,815 timeouts the logs place between rallies, Nebraska called 1,242. Nebraska won 794 of the rallies right after them, against 804 expected from the same situations without a timeout (p = 0.54). Opponents' 2,297 timeouts made no measurable difference either (p = 0.86).

What this record can't show

  • Each rally is printed as the one action that ended it: a kill, an error, an ace, a block. Passes, sets, digs and attacks that were dug are recorded nowhere, so nothing here measures them.
  • Opponents appear only in their matches against Nebraska, so nothing here is a scouting report on anyone else.
  • Rates on small samples come with wide intervals; read the interval, not just the number.

How it's built

Every official box score from 2001 on is archived as published, parsed, and checked against its own printed totals before it counts (879 box scores). A rally log is used only if every set passes six checks: rallies equal the final score, the score moves one point at a time, the serve changes on every side-out, the server follows the rotation, the final matches the box score, and the points by type match the box score. Where a school's site publishes the same match, the two logs are compared rally for rally. Every one of these checks held when this page was built.

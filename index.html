Momentum: scoring runs aren't what they look like

After winning three rallies in a row, Nebraska wins the next one 49.5% of the time — less than its 55.8% on all 86,174 rallies. That isn't a cold streak. The team that wins a rally serves the next one (true on 84,263 of 84,263 consecutive rallies), and serving is the harder side of the net. Winning hands the serve over.

Compare like with like — serves that follow a streak against serves that start a fresh turn, after taking out what a random order of the same rallies produces — and what is left is +0.5 points (95% interval -0.5 to +1.6). The record is big enough to catch an effect of about 1.3 points; it doesn't find one.

Runs of three or more points. Shuffling each set's own rallies into a random order 2,000 times (keeping every final score) produces more runs than really happened, for both teams: winning a rally hands over the serve, which breaks runs up.

Serving harder doesn't pay

Over 47,532 Nebraska serves in 26 seasons, 5.2% were aces and 8.2% errors. When a serve lands and isn't an ace, Nebraska still wins the rally 48.8% of the time — so a missed serve throws that away, and one extra point of error rate has to buy about 0.95 points of ace rate just to break even.

Nebraska's servers get nowhere near it. Comparing servers with each other, the riskier ones buy about 0.33 (95% interval 0.17 to 0.29); following one server from one season to the next, about 0.16 (0.06 to 0.27). Against a serve that never missed and never aced, the serving as a whole is worth -0.013 points a serve — about -633 points over 26 seasons.

How the game changed

Rally scoring arrived in 2001 and the 25-point set in 2008, but the set only changed length once.

Under side-out scoring only the serving team could score, so a set had as many rallies as a rally-scoring set to 30 but less than half the points.

Matches have also slowed down. Nebraska's matches ran at 1.72 rallies a minute in 2000 and 1.43 in 2026 (552 matches print both a duration and a full rally log).

Seasons without a figure are seasons whose box scores print no match duration.

On the bench

When does Nebraska substitute? In the 100 sets of Busboom Kelly 2025-2026 whose substitutions the record lets us read, Nebraska made 418 substitutions. 253 were planned rotation swaps — the same specialist in and out at the same turn of the rotation, set after set — and 84 came with Nebraska up 6 or more late in a set or a set from the match. That leaves 21 like-for-like changes, the only ones that could be a reaction to how a set was going. None of 7 possible triggers the record can see — a player's own errors just before, an opponent run, a stretch of failed side-outs, the score — shows up at those moments more than chance allows once 7 tests are allowed for. The closest is the score itself: Nebraska's margin in the set, in points (2.2 at these changes against 4.0 at comparable moments, p = 0.0087, just short of the 0.0071 needed). With so few, that rules out a big, regular trigger, not a subtle one. The same test does find triggers in Cook 2016-2024 (41 like-for-like changes), so it can find one when one is there.

Do timeouts stop a run? Of the 3,845 timeouts the logs place between rallies, Nebraska called 1,245. Nebraska won 796 of the rallies right after them, against 807 expected from the same situations without a timeout (p = 0.52). Opponents' 2,315 timeouts made no measurable difference either (p = 0.92).

What this record can't show

  • Each rally is printed as the one action that ended it: a kill, an error, an ace, a block. Passes, sets, digs and attacks that were dug are recorded nowhere, so nothing here measures them.
  • Opponents appear only in their matches against Nebraska, so nothing here is a scouting report on anyone else.
  • Rates on small samples come with wide intervals; read the interval, not just the number.

How the data is checked

Nothing counts until it passes its checks, and the build stops if any check fails.

  • Box scores. Every official box score from 2000 on is archived exactly as published, then parsed (880 so far, none failed to parse). Each team's player lines must add up to the printed team totals in all nine stat columns before they are used: 1,736 of 1,760 team tables do, and the rest are left out rather than guessed at.
  • Results. Every result is checked against the season's schedule page: 879 compared, 0 disagreeing. Season records add up to what the official season reports print in 26 of 26 seasons.
  • Rally logs. A play-by-play set is used only if it passes six checks: the rallies equal the final score, the score moves one point at a time, the serve changes on every side-out, the server follows the rotation, the final matches the box score, and the points by type match the box score. Of 2,266 sets read from every source, 1,911 are used; the rest failed a check or repeat a match already counted from another source.
  • A second source. Where the opponent's site publishes its own log of the same match, the two are compared rally by rally (108 matches; 6 differ somewhere, and each difference is looked at).
  • Corrections in the open. When a source is wrong (a misprinted jersey, a name spelled three ways, a table that does not add up), the fix is written down as its own line with the reason and the evidence, never made silently: 95 so far, 85 marked certain and 10 likely.
  • Readers. When someone spots a mistake, it is checked against the source and fixed, and the check that missed it is tightened so it cannot come back.

Every one of these checks held when this page was built.

The pipeline, the checks and much of this page's wording were written with an AI model (Anthropic's Claude) and reviewed by the one person who runs it. No number on the page comes from the model: each is computed by that code from the records above, and the tests compare the page's headline numbers with the files they come from.

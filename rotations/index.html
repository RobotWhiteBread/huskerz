The rules that make rotations work

1. You only rotate after a side-out

Rotation is triggered by one event: your team wins a rally that the other team served. The moment that happens, all six players move one spot clockwise and the player who was in zone 2 walks back to serve.

If you win a point on your own serve, nobody moves. The same server keeps serving, which is why Bergen Reilly can serve a 10-0 run (as she did in the 2026 home opener against New Mexico) without the lineup changing.

2. Clockwise, always

The order is 2 → 1 → 6 → 5 → 4 → 3 → 2. Think of it as a wheel turning clockwise when you look down at the court: right front drops back to serve, the server slides to middle back, middle back to left back, left back steps up to left front, left front to middle front, middle front to right front.

Because the six spots are a loop, every player is always directly opposite the same teammate. The setter is opposite the opposite hitter (that's where the name comes from), the two outside hitters face each other, and the two middles face each other. The dashed lines on the court above (in the At the serve view) connect those pairs.

3. The serving order is locked in by the lineup sheet

Before every set the coach hands the officials a lineup sheet with six jersey numbers in serving order. Slot 1 serves first if Nebraska starts with the ball; otherwise slot 1 is the first to serve after the first side-out. Substitutes inherit the slot of the player they replace, so the order never changes mid-set.

Coaches choose the starting rotation deliberately. Starting with the setter in zone 1 puts three attackers in the front row right away. Starting one spot earlier or later can put a specific server at the line first, or let the team spend more of the set in its strongest rotations.

4. The overlap rule: only at the moment of the serve

At the instant the ball is contacted for serve, both teams must be in rotational order. Each front-row player needs at least part of a foot closer to the net than the back-row player behind her (4 in front of 5, 3 in front of 6, 2 in front of 1), and the left-to-right order has to hold within each row.

That is the whole restriction. As soon as the serve is hit, players can go anywhere, subject to the front-row and back-row rules below. This is why you see hitters and the setter sprinting to new spots right after the toss: they line up legally, then "switch" to their real positions. The Serve receive view above shows the legal stack, and After the serve shows where everyone ends up.

5. Front row and back row have different powers

Players in zones 4, 3 and 2 are front-row players. They may block, and they may attack a ball at any height from anywhere.

Players in zones 5, 6 and 1 are back-row players. They may not block, and if they attack a ball that is above the top of the net, they must take off from behind the 3 m (10 ft) attack line. That back-row swing is the "pipe" or "bic" you hear announcers mention; Virginia Adriano showed she can hit it as a freshman.

6. The libero is a rotation shortcut

The libero wears a contrasting jersey and is a back-row-only player. She can replace any back-row teammate between rallies, as many times as needed, without it counting as a substitution. Almost every team uses her for the middle blockers, whose job is at the net: when a middle rotates to the back row, the libero goes in for her; when the middle is due back in the front row, she returns.

Limits: the libero cannot block, cannot attack a ball that is above the net at contact, and cannot set with her hands in front of the attack line if the ball is then attacked above the net. NCAA rules let her serve, but only in one spot in the serving order per set. So a team lets its libero serve for one middle, and the other middle either serves herself or gives the turn to a substitute. In 2026 Olivia Mauch serves in Manaia Ogbechie's spot and Andi Jackson usually serves her own.

7. Substitutions: 15 per set in college

NCAA teams get 15 substitutions per set (international rules allow only six), so a college coach can afford a defensive specialist who subs in for a middle blocker's back-row trip, then subs back out. A substitute is paired with the player she replaced and must re-enter for that same player.

In 2025 Laney Choboy and Olivia Mauch traded the libero jersey. In 2026 Mauch has worn it in every set, and Choboy is the defensive specialist: she comes in for the opposite, Virginia Adriano, when that spot reaches the back row, serves there, and goes out when it returns to the front.

8. 5-1 vs. 6-2: how many setters?

Nebraska runs a 5-1: one setter (Bergen Reilly) and five attackers. Reilly sets in all six rotations, which means the offense has three front-row attackers for three rotations and only two for the other three (when Reilly herself is in the front row).

A 6-2 uses two setters placed opposite each other. Each one sets from the back row and attacks from the front row, so the team always has three front-row hitters, at the cost of consistency and substitutions. Campbell Flynn, Nebraska's second setter, ran the offense against Grand Canyon in 2025 when the staff tried an unusual starting lineup.

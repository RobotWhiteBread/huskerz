Follow-up questions

Seven follow-up questions, each answered with one comparison fixed before it was run. They draw on 880 box scores (2000–2026) and 86,174 rallies from 620 matches with play-by-play. The answers lean on 14 tests in all, so a result counts only below p = 0.00357: seven questions asked of one record: a p-value under 0.05 somewhere among them is expected by chance, so each is held to 0.05 divided by the number of tests.

Question one

Does a set carry into the next one, beyond what the two teams' strength already predicts?

No: across 880 matches, the margin of one set says nothing about the next that a reordering of the same sets does not already say (p = 0.25).

Nebraska won the next set 85.9% of the time after winning one (84.7% if order did not matter) and 62.8% after losing one (63.9%).

AfterSetsWon the next setIf order did not matter
a set Nebraska won1,600 85.9%84.7%
a set Nebraska lost457 62.8%63.9%

After a set won by 8+ points, the next set's margin averaged +7.47 points against +7.38 if order did not matter (p = 0.468).

How it was tested, and what it cannot say

Each match's own sets are the comparison. Every order of its non-deciding sets that is still a possible match (the last one won by the match winner) is equally likely if sets do not carry over; the observed order is compared with the exact mean and variance over those orders. A deciding set stays in place and is left out of the pairs.

  • Set scores only: a carry-over that shows inside a set and not in its margin is not seen.
  • A 3-0 sweep has one winner throughout, so it informs the margin test and not the same-winner test.
  • One team's matches. The opponents are different every night; the reordering holds each night fixed.
Question two

Do servers change under pressure: at set point, late in a close set, or right after a timeout?

Yes, in one place: right after the other team's timeout, Nebraska's service-error rate was 5.6% against 7.8% for the same servers on the same nights otherwise.

Serving for the set: service-error rate 8.2% on 1,646 serves, 7.5% for the same servers on the same nights otherwise (p = 0.59).

Serving to stay in the set: service-error rate 8.0% on 301 serves, 7.6% for the same servers on the same nights otherwise (p = 0.93).

Late in a close set: service-error rate 7.3% on 1,174 serves, 8.2% for the same servers on the same nights otherwise (p = 0.13).

Right after the other team's timeout: service-error rate 5.6% on 2,104 serves, 7.8% for the same servers on the same nights otherwise (p < 0.0001).

Opponents' servers after a Nebraska timeout: service-error rate 7.8% on 1,022 serves against 7.0% otherwise (p = 0.58); ace rate 2.6% against 4.8% (p = 0.0057, short of the cutoff).

How it was tested, and what it cannot say

Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel: one server in one match, her serves in the state against her ordinary serves that night; the counts expected from her own ordinary rate are summed across servers and matches. Only a server with serves on both sides of the comparison contributes. The same match matters: a set is close late mostly because the opponent is good, and a good opponent is aced less all night. Timeouts are read only in sets whose source places a timeout line among the rallies, and a serve after a timeout is compared with the same server's serves at the same place in a service turn (first, second, third or later), because a timeout is called on a server who is already on a run.

  • Who serves at set point is not random. Comparing each server with herself on the same night removes that; it does not remove a coach choosing a serving substitute for the moment.
  • A match that reaches a late, close score is one in which the earlier rallies went a particular way. That selection can move the 'won' rows a little; it has no clear direction for errors and aces.
  • An ace and a service error are all the record says about a serve. A safer serve that stays in and is passed well looks the same as any other serve in play.
  • Servers are matched on the name as printed within one match.
Question three

How many matches until a team number is more signal than noise?

service errors per set: about 7 matches; hitting percentage: about 8 matches; digs per set: about 10 matches; aces per set: about 12 matches; kills per set: about 14 matches; share of all points won: about 17 matches; point-scoring percentage (rallies won on serve): about 24 matches until an average is half signal.

Seasons cannot be told apart at all in: opponent hitting percentage, blocks per set, side-out percentage. Nebraska's level in these has been too steady from year to year for any number of matches to rank one season over another.

Rotations: in 5 of 19 seasons the six rotations' side-out rates could be told apart by the end of the season (about 0.9 expected by chance alone).

How it was tested, and what it cannot say

For each number: its match-to-match variance inside a season, and the variance of season levels once the part that is only sampling is taken out. Their ratio is the number of matches at which an average is half signal; after n matches the share that is signal is n / (n + that). The early-against-rest correlations are the plain check: across seasons, the first n matches against all the later ones.

  • Match-to-match spread includes the opponent: a number moves because the other team was better, not only by chance. That is part of why early numbers mislead, and it is counted here as noise.
  • The first matches of a season are non-conference, so early-against-rest also carries a change in opposition.
  • Seasons from 2008 only, so every set is to 25 and per-set counts mean the same thing throughout.
  • A number whose seasons cannot be told apart at all (matches dealt to seasons at random spread as much, p of 0.05 or more) has no stabilisation point and is listed as none. That says the program's level in it has been steady, not that the number is meaningless in a match.
Question four

Is there a home-court edge once the opponent is held fixed, and does the size of the crowd matter?

Yes: against the same opponent in the same season (195 pairs), Nebraska's share of points was 2.26 points higher at home (1.62 to 2.89, p < 0.0001).

Away crowd size: no measurable link with how much worse Nebraska did there (rank correlation -0.10, p = 0.15).

Unadjusted, for comparison: home 58.0% of points over 451 matches; away 55.5% of points over 298 matches; neutral 55.6% of points over 91 matches. The unadjusted gap mixes venue with a softer home schedule.

Home crowd. Not tested: home matches sell out, so attendance barely moves inside a season; there is nothing to compare.

How it was tested, and what it cannot say

The unadjusted rows mix venue with opposition (the home schedule is softer). The test takes every opponent Nebraska met both at home and away in the same season and differences the two matches, so the opponent and both rosters are the same. Rally-scoring seasons only.

  • The paired opponents are conference opponents, so this is the home edge in conference play.
  • Two matches against one opponent are weeks apart; either team may have changed between them. That adds noise to each pair, not a direction.
  • Crowd size at an away match goes with the quality of the home team, which the pairing already holds; it also goes with how much that school cares about the match, which it does not.
Question five

Does Nebraska play worse on the second night of back-to-back matches?

No: on a second night, against an opponent it also met rested, Nebraska's share of points differed by -0.21 points (-1.48 to +1.06, p = 0.75; 49 such opponents).

Night two against the night before, different opponents (166 weekends): -1.33 points (p = 0.018). That comparison does not hold the opponent fixed, and the second match of a weekend is often the harder one; the answer above does.

How it was tested, and what it cannot say

Days since the previous match come from the schedule page. The test takes every opponent met twice in one season and regresses the difference in point share on the difference in venue (home or not) and the difference in rest (second night or not), so the opponent is the same in both halves of each pair.

  • In conference play the opponent is usually on a second night too. This measures whether Nebraska fares worse relative to its opponent, not whether both teams are tired.
  • The schedule is not random: who is played on which night is set by the conference.
  • Two matches on one date are left out, because the schedule page does not say which came first.
Question six

Does it matter who serves first in a set?

No: Nebraska won 79.9% of 723 sets it served first and 79.2% of 689 it received first; against reorderings of each match's own results, p = 0.70.

Measured edge to serving first: +0.7 points of set-win share (-2.7 to +4.0). The arithmetic predicts -1.3 at Nebraska's rates and -3.9 between two even teams.

Non-deciding setsSetsWonMean margin
Nebraska served first72379.9% +5.56
Nebraska received first68979.2% +5.30

First serve alternated in 989 of 989 back-to-back sets, which is what makes each match its own experiment.

How it was tested, and what it cannot say

First serve alternates from set to set, so inside one match Nebraska serves first in about half the sets and receives first in the rest. The serve-first pattern is held where it was and the match's set results are reordered in every valid way, as in block 1; the sets won (and the points margin) in serve-first sets are compared with the exact mean over those orders. The prediction is a plain calculation from side-out and point-scoring rates.

  • The deciding set has its own coin toss and is reported, not tested: a team that wins the toss may choose, so who serves first in it is not an accident.
  • A match with a set missing from the play-by-play is left out, because its orders cannot be listed.
  • The prediction assumes every rally is an independent draw at fixed rates. Between two even teams it gives the receiving side a few points of set-win probability; for a team as far ahead of its opponents as Nebraska it gives about one. This many sets can rule out a large effect and cannot confirm one that size.
Question seven

Did the libero (2002) or the relaxed double-contact rule (2024) change the numbers?

The double-contact rule (2024): larger than every other year-to-year change in rallies that ended on a ball-handling error, per 1,000 (9.23 to 0.00).

The printed season totals agree: ball-handling errors per set over both teams went from 0.26 to 0.02.

The libero (2002): no number moved more than it does in an ordinary year.

How it was tested, and what it cannot say

For each number, the season average over both teams in every Nebraska match, then the change from the season before the rule to the season of the rule. That change is set beside every other year-to-year change in the same scoring era: a rule matters here only if its year stands out from ordinary years, which move with rosters and schedules.

  • Both teams are counted, but every match has Nebraska in it. This is the rule as seen from one schedule.
  • One season before against one season after: a change that took several seasons to show is understated.
  • With about a dozen ordinary years to compare with, the smallest p a single number can reach is one over that count; this block can flag a change, not prove one.
  • The year the libero was allowed to serve is not tested: the project has not confirmed it against the rulebook.

When, and why, does the coach substitute?

Busboom Kelly 2025-2026, with Cook 2016-2024 as the comparison. Every number below is read from bench_stats.json.

The answer

No — that's all we find. In the 100 sets of Busboom Kelly 2025-2026 whose substitutions the record lets us read (of 142 trusted sets), Nebraska made 418 substitutions. 253 (60%) were planned rotation swaps — the same back-row specialist or front-row platoon in and out at the same turn of the rotation, set after set. 84 (20%) came with Nebraska ahead by 6 or more late in the set, or one set from the match: the "give a reserve experience" sub. 60 (14%) changed position group outside that rotation pattern, or involve a player no file gives a position. That leaves 21 like-for-like subs — a player for a player of the same position group, with the score not in hand — the only ones that could be a reaction to how the match was going. Against matched moments when the same player stayed on court at the same stage of a set, none of the 7 candidate triggers the record can see (her own errors just before, an opponent run, a side-out slump, trailing, the margin, who is about to serve) is more common at those subs than chance allows. The nearest is Nebraska's margin in the set, in points (2.19 at the sub against 4.03, p = 0.0087): below 0.05 on its own, not below the 0.0071 that 7 tests require. With 21 like-for-like subs this is a small sample: it can rule out a large, regular trigger, not a subtle or occasional one.

The comparison is not empty. Under Cook 2016-2024, the 41 like-for-like subs did follow a visible trigger: the outgoing player made a terminal error (attack error, blocked attack, service error, ball-handling error, bad set or block error, or was aced) in the previous 6 rallies (42% of the subs against 12% of matched moments, p < 0.001); the opponent had just scored 3 or more in a row (15% of the subs against 5% of matched moments, p = 0.0047); Nebraska was behind in the set (49% of the subs against 19% of matched moments, p < 0.001); Nebraska's margin in the set, in points (-1.39 at the sub against 3.60, p < 0.001). So the method can find a reactive sub when there is one to find.

7 candidate triggers are tested, so each must reach p < 0.05 / 7 = 0.0071 (Bonferroni) to count; with no trigger at all, at least one of 7 independent tests falls below 0.05 by chance 30% of the time.

What the record can read

huskers.com PDFs print each substitution with the player in, the player out and the score, and log libero replacements on their own lines; Sidearm pages print one 'subs' line per stoppage that reads as in; out pairs with libero swaps mixed in (the set's libero is identified from the swaps themselves); StatCrew pairs names on some pages and names only the incoming player on others. A set is used only if its substitutions read as pairs and agree with who served: nobody serves while off court, comes in while on it or goes out while off it.

The four kinds of sub

  • (a) planned rotation swap: a back-row specialist or a front-row platoon in and out at the matching rotation, the slot in one row throughout, a pair that recurs in other sets
  • (b) blowout / experience: Nebraska well ahead late in the set, or one set from the match
  • (c) like-for-like: the same position group in and out, neither of the above
  • (other) other: a different position group, or a position no file records

Each like-for-like sub is set against every other moment of the same season when the same player was on court at the same stage of a set (early, middle, late) and was not subbed; the expectation is the average of those moments, one per sub, and the test adds the subs up (observed against expected, normal approximation, two-sided). Seven candidates are tested, so one p below 0.05 in seven is close to what chance alone gives.
